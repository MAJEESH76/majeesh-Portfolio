OpenRocket-Based Design and Validation of a Booster-Sustainer Rocket System

Objective:

The design is for a two-stage model rocket. Its goals are stable flight and reaching a target altitude. Reliable separation of stages is essential. A parachute ensures safe recovery. The design balances material efficiency with aerodynamic performance.  

Sources and related content

The problem statement:

Designing a two-stage model rocket presents the complex challenge of optimizing aerodynamic configurations and structural integrity to achieve both maximum altitude and reliable flight performance, while ensuring efficient recovery systems. This project focuses on developing such a system, utilizing specific component choices and placement to analyze and enhance flight stability, predict apogee, and implement effective recovery strategies, contributing to advancements in model rocketry design for educational and experimental purposes.

Design Logic:

The two-stage rocket is configured with a sustainer as the first stage and a booster as the second stage. Both stages utilize body tubes made of Paper, spiral kraft glassine, Estes avg, bulk (0.89...). The rocket design incorporates ogive nose cones to minimize drag. Key aerodynamic parameters include fin dimensions and placement, which are crucial for stability.

Flow Chart:

Rocket Design Flow Chart

Define Mission Objective: The mission is to design and analyze a two-stage rocket for stable, high-altitude flight. Key objectives include achieving target apogee, ensuring safe recovery, and evaluating staging efficiency. The project will also assess structural integrity under flight loads. Controlled descent via parachute is a critical goal.

Motor Configuration: The rocket uses a C6-7 motor in the sustainer stage for delayed ejection after thrust. A B6-0 booster motor provides initial lift with zero delay. Two A3-4 side motors support early launch stability with a 4-second delay.

Design Rocket Structure: Each component (nose cone, body tubes, fins, rings) is dimensioned and material is selected for optimal mass and strength. Components are chosen based on their material strength-to-weight ratio, with emphasis on modularity for easier stage separation and repairs.

Aerodynamic Analysis: CG (46 cm) and CP (60.5 cm) are calculated to ensure a stable design with a 1.4 cal stability margin, indicating it will fly straight without tumbling. This confirms that the rocket will fly straight, resisting aerodynamic disturbances. A swept trapezoidal fin design is adopted to reduce drag and increase dynamic stability.

Recovery System Design: Nylon parachute and elastic shock cords are integrated to safely decelerate and recover the rocket after apogee. A dual-deployment parachute system made of Ripstop nylon, paired with a long elastic shock cord, ensures the rocket’s safe descent post-apogee. The recovery sequence is triggered by the first ejection charge, enabling separation and deceleration of both stages independently.

Simulation Results: The rocket achieves a simulated apogee of 342 m, with a peak speed of 92.5 m/s and acceleration of 139 m/s², indicating high performance.

Launch Readiness Check: A final assessment before the real-world launch to verify structural integrity, stability, and recovery systems. Prior to real-world testing, structural integrity, stage connections, motor alignment, and deployment sequences are verified. Environmental conditions like wind, humidity, and launch pad safety are also considered to ensure optimal launch conditions.

Conclusion:

This project successfully demonstrates the design, simulation, and analysis of a two-stage model rocket using OpenRocket. From structural layout and staging to propulsion and recovery, each subsystem was carefully integrated to ensure aerodynamic stability and safe flight performance. The rocket achieved a predicted apogee of approximately 342 meters, validating the effectiveness of the booster-sustainer configuration.

A dual-deployment recovery system, utilizing Ripstop nylon parachutes and elastic shock cords, ensured a safe and controlled descent post-apogee. The flight simulation confirmed proper staging, stability margins, and recovery deployment, making the design both functional and reliable.

Overall, this project highlights the importance of integrated design thinking, simulation-driven validation, and system-level coordination in aerospace applications. It sets the foundation for further optimization and potential real-world testing.
